# Song DNA: what the evidence says

*Research brief for the Still "Song DNA" feature. It is built only from claims that a verification pass rated confirmed or partly-correct, with the corrections applied. Where the verifier changed a number, a study type or an attribution, the corrected version is used. Claims rated wrong or overstated are left out or restated in their corrected form.*

---

## 1. Short answer: is it "frequencies my brain likes"?

**Almost certainly not, in the sense of a special pitch or tuning.** The evidence points to four other things:

- **Relationships between notes and chords.** The key factor is how predictable or surprising a progression is, not the absolute frequency of any note (Cheung 2019; Gold 2019).
- **Your own history with the songs.** Familiarity, memories and nostalgia matter a lot. This is likely a major contributor to the peace these songs bring you. It has not been measured for you or for this repertoire, so it is a hypothesis, not a finding (Pereira 2011; Krumhansl & Zupnick 2013; Barrett 2010; Janata 2009).
- **Real reward in the brain.** Peak musical pleasure involves dopamine (Salimpoor 2011), and dopamine causally changes how much people enjoy music (Ferreri 2019). How strong that reward is depends on the listener and on how much they like the piece. It is not a property of the waveform.
- **A few low-level cues with modest evidence.** Slower tempo goes with lower arousal (Bernardi 2006). Roughness (harsh, beating sound) is the cue most consistently linked to instability or negative feeling across cultures (Athanasopoulos 2021; Milne et al. 2023). Mode, meaning major vs minor, is largely learned (McDermott 2016; Smit 2022).

**What "frequency" can honestly mean here:** many ordinary listeners remember the exact pitch level of recordings they know well (Levitin 1994, replicated with smaller effects across labs by Frieler et al. 2013). The original Kishore recordings may therefore feel "right" partly because you have memorised their pitch and tuning. That is memory, not a frequency the brain is built to prefer.

**432 Hz and Solfeggio:** 432 Hz rests on small pilot studies with borderline or mixed results. There is one tiny, unreplicated 528 Hz study (n = 9). Nothing supports "DNA repair" or "healing" frequency claims.

**Your Am–G–F–E intuition is real as a style feature, not as a brain law.** It is the i–bVII–bVI–V "Andalusian" progression:

- G and F come from natural minor (Asavari).
- The E major chord needs G#, the raised 7th of harmonic minor (the Kirwani colour).
- In the Andalusian system the E major chord can also act as a second "home" (Manuel 1989; Manuel 2002).

No study shows this progression is calming. Song DNA can count how often it appears, and Still's preference learner can test whether you prefer it.

## 2. What plausibly makes these songs feel peaceful and loved (ranked by evidence strength)

### Tier A: strong or well-replicated evidence about mechanisms (not specific to these songs)

**1. Musical pleasure is real reward-system activity.**
- Salimpoor et al. (2011) measured dopamine release in the striatum at peak pleasure. The caudate was active during anticipation and the nucleus accumbens at the peak. The PET sample was small (about 8, from a secondary report), and listeners chose their own music. https://doi.org/10.1038/nn.2726
- Ferreri et al. (2019) ran a double-blind, within-subject trial with n = 27. Levodopa raised pleasure and willingness to pay, and risperidone lowered them. Valence and arousal ratings did not change, and the authors warn against saying "taking dopamine increases musical pleasure". https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1811878116
- Mas-Herrero et al. (2014) describe specific musical anhedonia: some healthy people get almost no reward from music. Any recommendation must therefore be personal, never universal. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2014.01.068

**2. Music reduces stress-related outcomes modestly, regardless of style.**
- de Witte et al. (2020) pooled 104 RCTs (327 effect sizes, 9,617 participants). Effects were d ≈ 0.38 on physiology, d ≈ 0.46 on heart rate and d ≈ 0.55 on self-reported stress. The interventions were mostly calming or preferred music, so no specific style or frequency is singled out. https://doi.org/10.1080/17437199.2019.1627897
- Lee (2016) pooled 97 RCTs (about 9,147 participants). Pain fell by about 1.13 points on 0–10 scales (SMD −0.39 on other scales), and both emotional distress and opioid use dropped. Correction: most of the included trials were passive music listening (about 87 music-medicine vs 10 music-therapy trials), not music therapy. https://academic.oup.com/jmt/article/53/4/430/973764

**3. Roughness is more universal than consonance preference or major/minor.**
- McDermott, Lehr & Oxenham (2010) tested n = 265 across two cohorts. Preference for consonance tracked preference for harmonicity (integer-ratio spectra) and grew with instrument training. Aversion to beating was uncorrelated with consonance preference, so a roughness measure captures a separate dimension and is not a stand-in for consonance. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2010.04.019
- McDermott et al. (2016) found that Tsimane' listeners did not prefer consonant chords but did dislike roughness. https://doi.org/10.1038/nature18635 The "not innate" reading is contested by Bowling et al. (2017, Music Perception). McPherson et al. (2020) found that the perceptual fusion of integer-ratio intervals is universal while aesthetic preference is not.
- Athanasopoulos et al. (2021) tested n = 169 in the UK and NW Pakistan. Roughness signalled anger across cultures, while consonance and mode preferences depended on culture. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0244964
- Milne, Smit, Sarvasy & Dean (2023) found roughness associated with musical *instability* in all groups tested. This is the correct source for that finding, not Smit 2022. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10511120/
- Harrison & Pearce (2020) model consonance as interference plus harmonicity plus cultural familiarity. https://doi.org/10.1037/rev0000169

**4. Music is universal, and variation within a society is larger than variation between societies.**
- Mehr et al. (2019) covered 60 societies. The useful axes are arousal (tempo, loudness, density), tonality and repetition. Expect large differences between songs even within one artist. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aax0868

### Tier B: moderate evidence, directly relevant to Song DNA

**5. Expectation: predictability combined with surprise.**
- Cheung et al. (2019) modelled about 80,000 chords from 745 US Billboard songs (about 1958–1991). Pleasure peaked for surprising chords after a predictable context, or predictable chords after an uncertain one. Amygdala, hippocampus and auditory cortex tracked this interaction, while the nucleus accumbens tracked uncertainty alone. Caveats: melody, rhythm and lyrics were stripped out, the music was Western, and participant n is unverified (press reports give about 80). https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2019.09.067
- Gold et al. (2019), with n = 43 and n = 27 using artificial melodies, found an interaction between information content and entropy, plus inverted-U relationships for each. https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.0428-19.2019

**6. Familiarity.**
- Pereira et al. (2011) scanned n = 14 (from 27 in a behavioural test) using pop/rock music. Familiar music engaged limbic and reward regions more than liking alone did. This is a small sample, so treat it as preliminary to moderate. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0027241
- Madison & Schiolde (2017) studied n = 15 over about 4 weeks with 40 excerpts heard 28 times each. Liking rose with exposure at every complexity level, with no inverted U. The "strongest predictor" was familiarity with *similar music* (genre or style familiarity), not repetition of the same piece. Preliminary. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnins.2017.00147

**7. Memory, reminiscence and nostalgia.**
- Krumhansl & Zupnick (2013) tested 62 US college students on 110 Billboard songs from 1955 to 2009. They found reminiscence bumps for music from the listeners' parents' youth and a smaller one for the 1960s. The authors offer two explanations: cross-generational transmission, or the quality of the music itself. Applying this to Hindi film music is an untested inference. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797613486486
- Janata (2009) scanned 13 participants with 30 excerpts each. The dorsal medial prefrontal cortex tracked both autobiographical salience and the music's movement through tonal space. Participants were pre-selected for frequent music-triggered memories, which inflates the effect. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2758676
- Barrett et al. (2010) is corrected here: it was not experience sampling. 226 undergraduates rated 30 random excerpts each, drawn from 1,112 songs. 26% of excerpts were rated at least somewhat nostalgic. Nostalgia mixed joy and sadness and was stronger for autobiographically salient, familiar and arousing songs. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0019006

**8. Sad-sounding or minor music can be pleasurable.**
- Sachs, Damasio & Habibi (2015) is a review. Liked sad music is pleasurable when it is not experienced as threatening, when it is aesthetically rewarding, and when it helps with mood regulation or empathy. It concerns *perceived sadness*, not minor mode as such. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2015.00404

**9. Tempo drives arousal, and there is no evidence that heart rate locks onto the beat.**
- Bernardi, Porta & Sleight (2006) tested n = 24 (12 musicians, 12 non-musicians). Faster music raised breathing rate, heart rate and blood pressure, and a 2-minute pause lowered them below baseline. Tempo was not manipulated independently of musical style. https://doi.org/10.1136/hrt.2005.064600
- Koelsch & Jäncke (2015) found no studies showing heart rate entraining to musical beats. https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehv430

**10. Choice and agency make music more comforting.**
- Garza-Villarreal et al. (2017) pooled 14 small, heterogeneous RCTs in chronic pain. Pain and depressive symptoms fell, and a subgroup comparison suggests a bigger effect when patients chose the music. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29149141/
- Howlin, Stapleton & Rooney (2022) ran a preregistered randomised 2×2 study with n = 286. Perceived choice predicted larger drops in pain intensity, especially in highly engaged listeners. Music complexity had no effect, and there was no no-music control. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0271329

**11. Cross-cultural emotion cues: tempo and complexity carry over better than mode.**
- Fritz et al. (2009) tested 21 Mafa listeners in Cameroon, who recognised happy, sad and scary Western music above chance. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2009.02.058
- Balkwill & Thompson (1999) had 30 Western listeners rate 12 Hindustani raga excerpts; they relied on tempo and complexity.
- Smit et al. (2022) tested 170 people in Papua New Guinea plus Sydney groups. The group with minimal Western exposure showed negligible evidence that major sounds happier than minor; groups with more exposure did. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0269597
- Mathur et al. (2015) tested 122 Indian listeners on 12 ragas. Tonic intervals predicted valence (the minor second predicted negative valence), and tempo and rhythmic regularity drove arousal. This is the closest evidence for this audience, and it argues for a profile of scale degrees, not just a major/minor label. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00513

**12. The voice is special.**
- Weiss, Trehub & Schellenberg (2012) found that sung melodies are remembered better than the same melodies on piano, banjo or marimba. Turning this into a "vocal presence" comfort axis is a design inference. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797612442552

### Tier C: evidence about Hindi film songs themselves (descriptive, not causal)

**13. Scale distribution of hits.**
- Choudhury, Bhagwan & Bali (ISMIR 2013) labelled 310 chart hits from 1953 to 2013, using only the mukhda.
- Asavari (natural minor) and Bilawal (major) are each about a quarter of hits, and about half together, in every decade.
- Bhairavi, Kafi and Khamaj together make up about another quarter.
- Kalyan is 4% and absent after the 1980s. Kirwani (harmonic minor) appears from the 1970s. Pentatonic scales are about 8%.
- Caveats: only 50 songs were double-labelled (with full agreement), and the rest by one expert. Songs were sampled from Binaca Geetmala years plus extra songs for selected composers. There is no comparison set of non-hits.
- https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2013/paper/000103.pdf

**14. Composer diversity matters more than singer.**
- Same paper, corrected figures: Kalyanji-Anandji used 11 distinct scales in 25 songs, R.D. Burman 10 in 38, and A.R. Rahman 10 in 30. Burman did not have the widest range.
- Of 16 Burman songs from 1971–73, 13 were Asavari, Bilawal or Nayaki, and the authors describe them as Western-pop influenced.
- Burman and Kalyanji-Anandji both wrote many Kishore songs, so expect a diverse scale profile rather than one "Kishore scale".

**15. Form: mukhda (refrain) plus one to four antaras (verses).**
- The authors' claim that the mukhda "drives success" is an assumption, not a measurement (Choudhury et al. 2013).

**16. The sound comes from arrangers and studio orchestras.**
- Booth (2008, OUP) is an oral history of arrangers and studio musicians from roughly the 1940s to the 1990s. https://academic.oup.com/book/9721
- Morcom (2007) treats songs in the context of their films, scenes, commercial life and reception. https://pure.royalholloway.ac.uk/en/publications/hindi-film-songs-and-the-cinema

**17. No dedicated acoustic study of Kishore Kumar's voice was found.** This is absence of evidence, not evidence of absence.

## 3. Myths to avoid

| Myth | What the evidence says |
|---|---|
| "432 Hz / 528 Hz / Solfeggio is the frequency the brain likes" | 432 Hz rests on small pilots: Calamassi & Pomponi 2019 (about −4.8 bpm heart rate, p ≈ 0.05, published in a complementary and alternative medicine journal), a later nurse study with no significant heart-rate effect, and a 2025 trial with a median −3 bpm. For 528 Hz there is one n = 9 study in a low-tier journal (Akimoto 2018). The "DNA repair" claim has no support. A 432 vs 440 retune is 31.8 cents. |
| "Old Bollywood songs are tuned to a special frequency" | Measure the tuning offset and describe it. Tape or transfer speed drift and instrument tuning are plausible causes, but no source verifies them for this corpus. Do not treat the offset as a mechanism. |
| "Consonance and major chords please all humans" | Preference is largely shaped by culture (McDermott 2016; Smit 2022), though this is contested (Bowling 2017). Perceptual fusion of intervals is universal, but liking them is not (McPherson 2020). Roughness effects carry across cultures better. |
| "Minor = sad = not calming" | Liked sad-sounding music can be pleasurable and help regulate mood (Sachs 2015). Nostalgia mixes joy and sadness (Barrett 2010). Am–G–F–E can be comfort music. |
| "A tempo at resting heart rate makes your heart sync" | There is no evidence of heart rate entraining to beats (Koelsch & Jäncke 2015). Only the direction "slower tends to be calmer" is supported (Bernardi 2006). |
| "20 songs reveal the secret hit formula" | Audio features predict popularity weakly at best. Pachet & Roy (2008, 32,000 songs) failed. Interiano et al. (2018, about 500k UK songs) reached about 85% only by adding a "superstar" artist variable. Film, lyrics, radio play, stars and familiarity dominate. With no non-hits in the set, popularity cannot be explained at all. |
| "The Andalusian cadence is the secret formula of classic Bollywood" | No peer-reviewed count of it in Hindi film songs exists. Any frequency we report is our own measurement on n = 20. |
| "These songs are raga, so they're classical" | Film-hit scale distributions differ strongly from Hindustani classical music (Choudhury 2013). A scale is not a raga, because a raga has rules for phrases, emphasis and ornament. |
| "Music releases dopamine, so this song treats migraine or anxiety" | Reward depends on the person (Ferreri 2019; Mas-Herrero 2014). Stress effects are modest (de Witte 2020). For migraine, Koenig 2013 (n = 78) found music therapy no better than an attention placebo. Oelkers-Ax 2008 (n = 58) was exploratory, with an unblinded music arm. Make no treatment claim. |
| "A key finder gives the true key / chord charts are ground truth" | Even state-of-the-art key estimation on in-genre pop gets about 68–80% exactly right, with errors clustered on fifth and relative keys (Korzeniowski & Widmer 2018). Human annotators agree on chord roots only 76–94% of the time (Pauwels 2019). |
| "More dynamic range = more relaxing" | Release year dominates dynamics, and macro-dynamics (LRA) were largely unaffected by the loudness war (Deruty & Pachet 2015). Listening tests show no clear preference for less-compressed masters (Hjortkjær & Walther-Hansen 2014). |
| "Matching chords and tempo is copying" | Common building blocks such as chord progressions are generally not protectable (Sheeran 2024; Skidmore 2020; Gray v. Hudson 2022). However, "feel" is not categorically safe (Williams v. Gaye 2018), and melodies, lyrics and recordings are protected. |

## 4. What to measure in each song, and exactly how

The general rule is to analyse the **full length** of every song and keep per-frame and per-section values, not just song means. Every estimate is reported with a confidence and with alternatives.

### 4.0 Preprocessing
- **Offline (Python, librosa, ISC licence):** decode at 22.05 kHz. Analyse a mono sum and keep L/R for stereo and loudness. Trim leading and trailing audio below −60 dBFS. Record the file's source and remaster, because remasters change crest factor.
- **Browser (Web Audio `decodeAudioData`):** use 44.1–48 kHz, 4096-sample frames and a 1024–2048 hop.
- Ship only derived numbers to the product. Never ship or upload audio.

### 4.1 Tuning offset (do this first)
- **Why first:** Chuan & Chew (2012) found that 471 of 727 key-label mismatches in their set came from tuning practices. A transfer that is about 50 cents off can flip a detected key by a semitone.
- **Offline:** `librosa.estimate_tuning(y, sr, bins_per_octave=12) × 100` gives the offset in cents, in the range [−50, +50). Pass `tuning=` to `chroma_cqt`.
- **Browser:**
  1. Take an STFT (4096–8192 points) or a constant-Q transform at 36 or more bins per octave.
  2. Pick spectral peaks between 80 and 2000 Hz.
  3. Fold each peak to its cents deviation from the nearest A440 equal-tempered pitch.
  4. Take the magnitude-weighted circular mean.
- **Output:** offset in cents plus the implied A4 (e.g. "A4 ≈ 446 Hz"), labelled "recording/transfer tuning offset".
- **Note:** the Chroma Toolbox chooses only between coarse shifted filterbanks (about 25–33 cent steps). Use the continuous estimate instead.

### 4.2 Key and mode (Western view)
1. Compute HPSS (`librosa.effects.hpss`, margin 2–3), then `chroma_cqt` on the harmonic part with tuning correction. Optionally add a bass chroma from 30–250 Hz.
2. Average the chroma, weighted by duration, over the whole song and over each 16-beat or roughly 30-second window.
3. Pearson-correlate against all 24 rotations of **two** profile sets: Krumhansl-Kessler (https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.89.4.334) and Temperley 1999 (https://doi.org/10.2307/40285812).
4. Output the top 3 keys with r values, the margin r1 − r2, the share of windows that agree, and whether the two profile sets agree.
5. Flag the song as **"modal/ambiguous"** when the margin is small. Start at about 0.05 and tune the threshold on synthetic songs.
6. For Andalusian material, always show the alternative readings: A minor, C major (relative) and E Phrygian-dominant.

**Expected accuracy:** below the about 80% exact / 85% weighted figure on Billboard. That number came from a model trained on Billboard with keys derived heuristically, so it is optimistic. Untrained template methods on old Hindi film audio will do clearly worse (Korzeniowski & Widmer 2018). Classical-derived profiles also transfer poorly across genres (Faraldo et al. 2016).

### 4.3 Tonic and scale (Indian view, with raga caution)
- Report key as **tonic (Sa) plus scale**, e.g. "Sa ≈ A, −23 cents, Asavari", not just "A minor".
- **Method:**
  1. Build a tuning-corrected 12-bin chroma histogram over vocal-dominant frames, or over the most-repeated section (the mukhda), as Choudhury did.
  2. Rotate through the 12 possible tonics.
  3. Score each rotation by cosine similarity against these templates: Bilawal (major), Asavari (natural minor), Kafi (Dorian), Khamaj (Mixolydian), Bhairavi (Phrygian), Kalyan (Lydian), Kirwani (harmonic minor), Nayaki (minor hexatonic), and Bhupali/Shivaranjani (pentatonic).
- **Report:**
  - the top two scales with their margin
  - an **accidental ratio**: chroma mass outside the best-fit scale
  - the **7th-degree split**: G vs G# energy in A-minor songs, i.e. the natural-minor vs harmonic-minor colour that Am–G–F–E needs
  - a scale-degree (tonic-interval) profile, following Mathur 2015
- **Reference accuracy:** tonic identification on Indian *art* music (with a drone) reaches about 90% using multi-pitch analysis plus machine learning (Gulati 2014). Expect worse on orchestrated film audio. Show confidence and allow user correction.
- **Wording:** say "scale resembles raga X", never "is in raga X".

### 4.4 Chords
1. Compute beat-synchronous, tuning-corrected, HPSS-harmonic chroma, bass-weighted if possible. NNLS-style approximate-transcription chroma helps on difficult chords (Mauch & Dixon 2010).
2. Match against 24 major/minor triad templates plus "N" (no chord, low energy).
3. Smooth with Viterbi, using a sticky self-transition probability of about 0.8–0.95, tuned on synthetic songs. HMM smoothing beats filtering the chroma (Pauwels 2019).
4. Merge consecutive identical labels and keep the mean posterior for each segment as its confidence.
5. Convert chord roots to Roman numerals relative to the estimated tonic.

Keep the vocabulary at major/minor triads. A realistic target is about 70–80% agreement at the major/minor level, which needs validating. Label output as "Am-like", not ground truth.

### 4.5 Andalusian cadence detection and progression DNA
- **Template:** transposition-invariant root sequence **[0, 10, 8, 7]** relative to the minor tonic (i–bVII–bVI–V).
- **Also detect:**
  - rotations of the template
  - partial forms: i–bVII–bVI, bVI–V–i, bVI–V
  - whether the song *rests* on i or on V. If it rests on V, show the Phrygian-on-E reading, following Manuel's dual tonicity (https://doi.org/10.2307/767769; https://doi.org/10.1525/jams.2002.55.2.311).
- **Other named patterns:** I–V–vi–IV, i–iv–v, I–vi–IV–V, ii–V–I.
- **Per song:**
  - share of song time covered by each pattern
  - 2-, 3- and 4-gram root histograms
  - chord-change rate per bar
  - share of time on the tonic
  - use of a major V in minor
- **Expectation metrics (after Cheung 2019):** build first- and second-order transition matrices per artist with add-one smoothing. Score each song with a model trained on the other songs (leave-one-song-out). Report:
  - mean surprisal: −log₂ P(next chord)
  - mean entropy of the predictive distribution
  - "pleasure-zone share": the fraction of transitions that are low-entropy and high-surprise, or high-entropy and low-surprise
- **Caveat:** Cheung's model used Western chord sequences with no melody. Here it is a descriptor, not a pleasure prediction. Any Andalusian frequency we report is our own result on n = 20.

### 4.6 Tempo and metre
- Compute a spectral-flux onset envelope, then autocorrelation or a tempogram. The offline reference is `librosa.beat.beat_track` (Ellis's dynamic-programming method).
- **Report:**
  - median BPM **with its half-time and double-time alternatives**, e.g. "72 or 144". Accuracy above 80% is reached only when the metrical level is forgiven (Gouyon 2006, 3,199 instances).
  - tempo stability: standard deviation or IQR of local BPM over 10-second windows
  - pulse clarity: ratio of the tempogram peak
  - beat grouping: 4-beat (Keherwa-like) vs 3 or 6-beat (Dadra-like), from autocorrelation peaks at 3 vs 4 beats
- **Comparing songs:** use the octave-folded log₂ tempo ratio, wrapped to [−0.5, 0.5].
- Restrict the search to roughly 60–180 BPM. Any prior over that range is a design heuristic, not an evidence-based "healing" BPM.

### 4.7 Dynamics
Implement exactly per EBU Tech 3342 / ITU-R BS.1770 (https://tech.ebu.ch/publications/tech3342):
- K-weighting.
- Short-term loudness on 3-second windows at 10 Hz or more.
- **LRA** = P95 − P10 of short-term loudness, after a −70 LUFS absolute gate and a **−20 LU** relative gate. LRA uses −20 LU. Do not confuse it with the −10 LU integrated-loudness gate, or with the stray "−8 to −10" line in the document history.
- Integrated LUFS, sample or true peak, crest factor (peak-to-RMS in dB, median per 3-second window), and PLR (true peak minus integrated LUFS).
- Short-term loudness percentiles p10, p50 and p95.
- A section-level energy curve, either in 20 normalised segments or per detected section.
- **Build-up arc** (after Salimpoor 2011): the slope of loudness and onset density in the 4–8 bars before each mukhda return.
- **Unit tests:** EBU conformance signals expect LRA = 10, 5, 20, 15, 5 and 15 LU, each ±1.
- **Era caution:** in the Western corpus studied, 1960s–80s masters differ from modern ones mainly in **crest factor and PLR, not LRA** (Deruty & Pachet 2015, 7,200 tracks, 1967–2014). Compare within era, and record the remaster.

### 4.8 Spectrum, timbre and roughness
- Long-term average spectrum in 1/3-octave bands, normalised to 0 dB at 1 kHz.
- Energy shares: <120 Hz, <250 Hz, 250 Hz–2 kHz, 2–6 kHz and >6 kHz. Compare these against Still's existing comfort-zone bands.
- Spectral centroid (median and IQR), 85% rolloff, flatness.
- Era and transfer effects will dominate the high bands, so normalise or flag them before comparing across eras.
- **Roughness proxy:** per sustained (non-onset) frame, take the top 10–20 spectral peaks and sum the pairwise Plomp-Levelt / Sethares dissonance weighted by the product of amplitudes. Report per section.
- **Harmonicity:** harmonic-template fit or harmonic-to-noise ratio on the dominant melodic or vocal line.
- Report roughness and harmonicity as *separate* descriptors (McDermott 2010). Check them against the `incon` reference models (https://github.com/pmcharrison/incon).

### 4.9 Stereo
- Per 3-second window: mid/side energy ratio S/(M+S), inter-channel Pearson correlation, and per-band side energy.
- Flag **"effective mono"** when correlation stays above about 0.98. Many period transfers may be mono or pseudo-stereo, which is unverified per file.
- Exclude width from similarity scoring for mono-flagged songs. Width is a period cue, not a quality score.

### 4.10 Vocal presence
- **Browser estimate:** per frame, compute the mid-channel energy in 300 Hz–3.4/4 kHz relative to the full band, and require a voiced f0 (YIN or autocorrelation, about 80–1000 Hz, high clarity) together with low spectral flatness.
- **Report:**
  - vocal-band active %
  - median vocal f0
  - vocal range in semitones
  - vibrato rate (4–7 Hz modulation of f0) where it can be detected
  - singer-formant share (about 2–4 kHz)
  - difference between vocal sections and interludes. Arranger-led interludes should show higher spectral flux and less vocal-band energy (Booth 2008).
- **Label:** "vocal-band presence (estimate)".
- **Optional offline ground truth:** Hybrid Transformer Demucs (MIT code). Its about 9.2 dB SDR needs extra training data. Keep separated stems local and delete them afterwards.

### 4.11 Repetition and structure
- Build a beat-synchronous self-similarity matrix from chroma plus MFCC (cosine).
- Apply Foote checkerboard-kernel novelty with an 8–16 beat kernel and peak-pick boundaries.
- **Repetition ratio:** the share of beats whose best off-diagonal match exceeds cosine 0.8 at a lag of at least 8 beats.
- Use a lag matrix to find the mukhda (the most-repeated, vocal-heavy segment), the antara count and the share of instrumental interludes.
- **Report:** number of sections, mukhda returns, antaras, interlude share, mean section length in bars.
- Do not store the matrix or any melody contour. Evaluate boundaries with `mir_eval.segment` at ±3 seconds. Approximate labels only (Paulus et al. 2010).

### 4.12 Metadata that audio can't see (store per song)
- Composer, lyricist, film, year, and scene mood (romantic, sad, playful, philosophical), following Morcom.
- Source and remaster of the file.
- User fields: familiarity (0–10), "nostalgic / memory-linked", "how I first heard it" (parents, radio, film), and the user's own mood tag.

## 5. How to validate the analysis

1. **Synthetic suite first, with pass thresholds fixed before touching real songs.**
   - Render about 50 songs with known key, tuning offsets (0, ±20, ±40 cents), tempo (70–160 BPM), progressions (Am–G–F–E, I–V–vi–IV, ii–V–I), drums and a vocal-like lead.
   - Score with `mir_eval` (key with MIREX weighting: correct 1, fifth 0.5, relative 0.3, parallel 0.2; chord root and majmin; beat; tempo; segment).
   - Example thresholds: weighted key score ≥ 0.8 and chord majmin ≥ 0.85 on synthetic songs.
   - Cite the `mir_eval` *library docs* for key and tempo metrics. The 2014 paper predates them.
2. **Era-matched benchmark.** Run on McGill Billboard (1958–1991) and Isophonics Beatles annotations, using audio the user owns, since annotations only are distributed. Check the licences on the dataset pages.
3. **Loudness conformance.** Pass the EBU Tech 3341/3342 test signals (±1 LU).
4. **Ear-check real songs.** Hand-annotate key, tonic and 60 seconds of chords for 3–5 Kishore songs. Publish the agreement rate. These are the only ground truth for Hindi film harmony, since no public chord or key dataset for it was found.
5. **Cross-check against reference tools offline.** Use Chordino / NNLS Chroma (GPL) for chords and librosa for tuning and tempo. Essentia is a reference only; see licensing in section 6.
6. **Profile sanity test.**
   - Hold out 3–5 Kishore songs and build the profile on the rest.
   - Contrast set: 3–5 songs by other 1970s Hindi artists (Rafi, Asha) and 3–5 modern or Western songs.
   - Expected: held-out Kishore songs rank closest, then the period artists, then the modern songs.
   - Report AUC with n stated. This is a sanity check, not proof of why the songs are loved.
7. **Separate the profile from familiarity.** Run blind pairwise comfort tests of *unfamiliar* songs that match the DNA against unfamiliar songs that don't, plus familiar songs that don't match, through Still's GP pairwise learner. Only then claim the profile adds anything beyond "I already know and love this".
8. **Report uncertainty everywhere.**
   - per-song key and chord confidence
   - agreement between the two key-profile sets
   - artist spread (IQR), not just medians
   - a 20-song caveat on every artist-level statistic

## 6. Feature design for Still

### 6.1 Artist profile ("Song DNA")
- For each descriptor, store the **median, IQR, n and per-song values**.
  - Use a circular distribution for key and tonic, an octave-folded distribution for tempo, and pattern-frequency tables for progressions.
  - Include the tuning-offset distribution.
- Group profiles by **composer** as well as singer. Kishore sang for Burman, Kalyanji-Anandji, Laxmikant-Pyarelal and others, and much of the scale DNA comes from the composer.
- Add **mood sub-profiles** (romantic, sad, playful) instead of pooling everything into one centroid.
- Version the profiles, and support adding Rafi, R.D. Burman, Asha Bhosle and Lata Mangeshkar as separate profiles plus a pooled era profile.
- Name it honestly: "shared style features of the songs you chose", not "what makes your brain happy" or "why they were hits".

### 6.2 Compare-a-song
- Analyse locally in the browser and keep only the derived JSON.
- For each feature, compute a robust z-score: (value − median) / (1.4826 × MAD). Combine them into a weighted distance and map it to a 0–100 similarity.
- Show a **per-dimension breakdown** (key/scale, progressions, tempo, dynamics, spectrum, form, vocal) with the 3 most similar and 3 most different features in plain words.
- Show a **confidence banner** when key or chord confidence is low or the key is modal or ambiguous. Let the user correct the key or tonic.
- Show **familiarity as a separate axis**. Never present a low similarity as "you won't like this". New songs tend to grow on people with repeated listening (Madison & Schiolde 2017).
- Make it situation-aware through Still's existing per-state comfort levels, for example a calm or pre-sleep state (Schedl et al. 2018).
- Never output a "popularity score" or a "scientifically calming" label.

### 6.3 Style sketch generator
- **Procedural and rule-based only:** Web Audio or Tone.js (MIT). Do not train or fine-tune any model on the songs.
- **Parameters, sampled from the profile:**
  - BPM within the artist's IQR, with a "slower" option
  - key and tonic (user-transposable)
  - scale
  - a progression loop drawn from the profile's Roman-numeral n-gram or Markov model, with the default or forced option of i–bVII–bVI–V
  - chord-change rate
  - a **surprise rate** of 0–15%, meaning borrowed or unexpected chords at phrase ends such as the antara start. This range is a design heuristic loosely inspired by Cheung 2019, and the user's ratings tune it.
  - form: intro, mukhda, interlude, antara, mukhda
  - Keherwa or Dadra-like percussion
- **Timbre and comfort:**
  - Use harmonic timbres (pads, plucked strings, piano).
  - Keep voicings above about C3 except the bass root, to limit low-register roughness.
  - Keep roughness at or below the profile's median.
  - Add 2–4 second silence gaps between sections (Bernardi 2006 found pauses lower arousal).
- **No melody imitation.** Any top line is a constrained random walk within the scale using stepwise motion, checked so it does not reproduce any 6-note-or-longer sequence from known material. No sampling and no voice cloning.
- **Be honest that sketches lack two ingredients:** the voice (Weiss 2012) and familiarity. Expect them to be liked less than the originals at first, and track ratings across repeat plays.
- **Original pitch:** the generator can transpose freely, and it can mention that the original pitch level may feel more familiar (Levitin 1994).
- **Present sketches as options** the user picks, vetoes or reorders, never auto-play. Agency matters (Howlin 2022).
- **432 Hz:** offer it only as a **blind A=432 vs A=440 A/B test** inside the preference learner, so the user gets personal data rather than a claim.
- **Objective sanity check per sketch:** tempo, mode, chord-transition entropy and loudness against the profile ranges, plus symbolic metrics (pitch-class and interval histograms, following Yang & Lerch 2020).

### 6.4 Learning personal taste
- Use the existing **GP pairwise learner** with blinded A/B trials:
  - sketch vs sketch differing in one parameter (Andalusian on or off, tempo, surprise rate, tuning), or sketch vs a neutral procedural control
  - loudness matched within ±1 LU
  - randomised order, hidden labels, 20–40 second clips
  - the question "Which feels more comfortable right now?" plus an optional 0–10 rating
- Require roughly 10–20 or more comparisons, and a 95% credible interval that excludes 0.5, before stating a preference.
- **Personal signals beside audio similarity:** play counts, familiarity, the nostalgia tag, the "first heard via" field and the user's mood tags. Recommendations should combine "sounds like your favourites" with "you already know it".
- **Optional:** the Barcelona Music Reward Questionnaire (Mas-Herrero 2013) to stratify how sensitive a user is to music reward. This was flagged by the verifier and is not yet independently checked.

### 6.5 Migraine safety
- Route every uploaded song preview and every sketch through Still's existing **safety limiter**.
- Default to low volume, with a fade-in of at least 3 seconds.
- Sketches have no sudden transients and are kept low in roughness.
- Phonophobia is common during attacks, so make "stop / quieter" one tap away.
- **Visible disclaimer:** "For comfort and enjoyment. Not a migraine treatment. Stop if sound worsens symptoms."
- If the app logs outcomes, use the user's own 0–10 comfort or pain rating before and after, compared against a quiet or no-music control session.
- Make no therapeutic, dopamine or healing-frequency claims. Point treatment questions to a clinician and a headache diary.

## 7. Copyright and ethics rules

1. **Audio stays local.**
   - Analyse in the browser or in an offline script on the user's machine.
   - Store only aggregate numeric descriptors.
   - Never upload, cache, redistribute or commit audio, separated stems, self-similarity matrices or melody contours to the repo.
2. **Never extract, store or regenerate melodies, lyrics, recognisable hooks or a specific song's section order.**
3. **Common building blocks only in sketches:** tempo, key, scale, generic progressions such as Am–G–F–E, metre and instrument palette.
   - US cases: chord progressions plus harmonic rhythm are too commonplace to protect (*Structured Asset Sales v. Sheeran*, 2d Cir. 2024). Commonplace elements such as descending scales, arpeggios and short note sequences are unprotectable, but selection and arrangement can be protected (*Skidmore v. Led Zeppelin*, 9th Cir. 2020 en banc). An ostinato built from commonplace elements is not protectable (*Gray v. Hudson*, 9th Cir. 2022).
   - Caution: *Williams v. Gaye* (9th Cir. 2018) upheld a verdict widely read as protecting "feel", so do not claim feel is categorically safe.
4. **No generative-AI training on the songs.**
   - The USCO Part 3 report (pre-publication, May 2025) says training is not categorically fair use and that market harm often decides.
   - Later decisions point both ways. *Bartz v. Anthropic* and *Kadrey v. Meta* (N.D. Cal., June 2025) found fair use on their facts. *GEMA v. OpenAI* (Munich, 11 Nov 2025) held that memorising and reproducing lyrics infringes, and a 2026 GEMA v. Suno ruling reportedly went against an AI music generator.
   - Procedural generation avoids this. If Magenta.js (Apache-2.0) is used later, document where its pretrained checkpoints came from.
5. **Labelling:** call outputs "an original sketch in a tempo/harmony style", never "a Kishore Kumar song". Never imitate or clone a singer's voice.
6. **Indian law is not yet analysed.** The songs are Indian recordings. The Copyright Act 1957 has narrower fair dealing (s.52), and labels such as Saregama hold most 1960s–80s catalogues. Get legal review covering India and the US before any commercial launch.
7. **Library licences:**
   - Meyda: MIT
   - Tone.js: MIT
   - librosa: ISC
   - mir_eval: MIT
   - pyloudnorm: MIT
   - Essentia / essentia.js: **AGPL-3.0**. Avoid it in the shipped product, or buy MTG's commercial licence.
   - Chordino / NNLS Chroma: GPL. Offline reference only.
   - Saraga: CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. Research only, not shipped data.
   - CompMusic tonic data: licence differs by copy.
8. **Honesty in copy:**
   - Call profiles descriptive, built from 20 songs the user chose.
   - Say that familiarity and memory are likely major contributors that audio cannot see.
   - Say that lyrics and meaning are out of scope (Brattico 2011, preliminary: lyrics weigh more in sad music).
